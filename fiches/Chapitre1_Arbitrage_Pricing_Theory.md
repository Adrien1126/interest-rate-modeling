# Chapter 1 — Introduction to Arbitrage Pricing Theory

> A short, pedagogical introduction adapted for learning.

---

This chapter introduces the minimal probabilistic and analytic tools needed for modern arbitrage pricing: a filtered probability space, Brownian motion (multi-dimensional), basic Itô processes, the no-arbitrage principle and equivalent martingale measures. The presentation is concise and aimed at intuition and practical use.

## 1.1 Notation and setup

- We work on a filtered probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\ge0}, \mathbb{P})$ satisfying the usual conditions (complete and right-continuous filtration).
- Expectations under $\mathbb{P}$ are denoted by $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]$ or simply $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$. Conditional expectation given $\mathcal{F}_t$ is $\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{F}_t]$ or $\mathbb{E}_t[\cdot]$.
- Time is continuous and nonnegative: $t\in[0,\infty)$.

### d-dimensional Brownian motion (Wiener process)

Definition (informal): A process $W=(W_t)_{t\ge0}$ with values in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a d-dimensional Brownian motion under $\mathbb{P}$ if:

1. $W_0=0$ almost surely.
2. Almost surely the paths $t\mapsto W_t(\omega)$ are continuous.
3. For $0\le s<t$, the increment $W_t-W_s$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$.
4. For $0\le s<t$, the increment $W_t-W_s$ has a multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance $(t-s)I_d$:

$$
W_t-W_s \sim \mathcal{N}\big(0, (t-s)I_d\big).
$$

Remarks:
- Each component $W^i$ is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion and different components are independent.
- More generally, one may consider a Gaussian process with covariance matrix $(t-s)\Sigma$ where $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive semidefinite. In that case one can write $W_t=\Sigma^{1/2}B_t$ where $B$ is standard $\mathbb{R}^d$-Brownian motion.

### Itô processes and asset prices

A d-dimensional Brownian motion is used to drive stochastic differential equations (SDEs). A (vector) Itô process $X=(X_t)_{t\ge0}$ in $\mathbb{R}^p$ has the form

$$
\mathrm{d}X_t = \mu(t,X_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(t,X_t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t,
$$

where $\mu:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^p$ is the drift and $\sigma:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}$ is the diffusion matrix. We assume $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are progressively measurable and satisfy growth and integrability conditions ensuring existence and uniqueness of solutions (Lipschitz or local Lipschitz and linear growth conditions in typical examples).

In finance, asset price vectors (discounted when needed) are modelled as Itô processes. For example, a single stock price under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ can be written as

$$
\mathrm{d}S_t = S_t\mu_t\,\mathrm{d}t + S_t\sigma_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t.
$$


> #### Definition 1.1.1 (Martingale)
> 
> Let $Y(t)$ be an adapted vector-valued process with $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(|Y(t)|)< \infty$ for all $t \in [0, T]$. We say that $Y(t)$ is a martingale under measure $\mathbb{P}$ if for all $0\le t\le s \le T$, 
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[Y(s)] = Y(t) \quad \text{a.s.}
> $$

> #### Definition 1.1.2 (Space $H^2$)
> 
> Let $\|\sigma(t,\omega)\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}(\sigma(t,\omega)\sigma(t, \omega)^\top)$. We say that $\sigma$ belongs to $H^2$ if, for all $t\in [0, T]$,
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_0^t \|\sigma(s,\omega)\|^2\,\mathrm{d}s\right] < \infty.
> $$

> #### Theorem 1.1.3 (Properties of the Itô integral)
> 
> Define $I(t) = \int_0^t \sigma(s,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}W_s$ and assume that $\sigma$ belongs to $H^2$. Then:
> 
> 1. $I(t)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable.
> 2. $I(t)$ is a continuous martingale. In particular, $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[I(t)] = 0$ for all $t\in[0,T]$.
> 3. $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[|I(t)|^2] = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_0^t \|\sigma(s,\omega)\|^2\,\mathrm{d}s\right] < \infty$.
> 4. $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[I(t)I(s)^\top] = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_0^{\min(t,s)} \sigma(u,\omega)\sigma(u,\omega)^\top\,\mathrm{d}u\right]$.


> #### Theorem 1.1.4 (Martingale Representation Theorem)
> 
> If $Y$ is a local martingale adapted to the filtration generated by a Brownian motion $W$, then there exists a process $\sigma$ such that:
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{d}Y_t = \sigma(t,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}W_t.
> $$
> 
> If $Y$ is a square-integrable martingale, then $\sigma$ belongs to $H^2$. 


> #### Theorem 1.1.5 (Itô's lemma)
> 
> Let $f(t,x)$, $x=(x_1,\dots,x_p)^\top$, denote a $C^{1,2}$ function $f : [0, T]\times \mathbb{R}^p \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. Let $X(t)$ be given by the Itô process above and define the scalar process $Y(t) = f(t, X(t))$. Then $Y(t)$ is an Itô process with stochastic differential
> 
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathrm{d}Y_t &= f_t(t, X_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \nabla_x f(t, X_t)^\top \mu(t, X_t)\,\mathrm{d}t \\
> &\quad + \nabla_x f(t, X_t)^\top \sigma(t, X_t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t \\
> &\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma(t,X_t)\sigma(t,X_t)^\top D^2_x f(t,X_t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.
> \end{aligned}
> $$

## 1.2 Trading Gains and Arbitrage

We now consider an investor engaging in a trading strategy involving the $p$ assets $X_1,\dots,X_p$. Let the trading strategy be characterized by a predictable adapted process $\phi(t,\omega) = (\phi_1(t,\omega),\dots,\phi_p(t,\omega))^\top$, with $\phi_i(t,\omega)$ denoting the holdings at time $t$ in the $i$-th asset $X_i$. The value $\pi(t)$ of the trading strategy at time $t$ is thus (dropping the dependence on $\omega$ in the notation)

$$
\pi(t) = \phi(t)^\top X(t).
$$

An investment strategy is said to be self-financing if, for any $t \in [0, T]$,

$$
\pi(t) - \pi(0) = \int_0^t \phi(s)^\top \,\mathrm{d}X_s.
$$

> #### Definition 1.2.1 (Arbitrage)
> 
> An arbitrage opportunity is a self-financing strategy $\phi$ for which $\pi(0)=0$ and, for some $t\in[0,T]$,
> 
> $$
> \pi(t) \ge 0 \quad \text{a.s.,} \quad \text{and }\; \mathbb{P}(\pi(t)>0)>0.
> $$

## 1.3 Equivalent Martingale Measures and Arbitrage

> #### Theorem 1.3.1 (Radon–Nikodym theorem)
> 
> Let $\mathbb{P}$ and $\widehat{\mathbb{P}}$ be equivalent probability measures on the common measurable space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$. There exists a unique (a.s.) non-negative random variable $R$ with $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[R]=1$ such that, for all $A\in\mathcal{F}$,
> 
> $$
> \widehat{\mathbb{P}}(A) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[R\,\mathbb{1}_A].
> $$
> 
> For later use, we associate any probability measure $\widehat{\mathbb{P}}$ with a density process
> 
> $$
> \zeta(t) = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right], \quad \forall t\in[0,T].
> $$
> 
> Clearly, $\zeta(t)$ is a $\mathbb{P}$-martingale with $\zeta(0)=1$ and $\zeta(t)=\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[\zeta(T)]$.
> 
> A simple conditioning exercise demonstrates that for any $\mathcal{F}_T$-measurable random variable $Y(T)$, with $R=\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}$,
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{E}^{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}[Y(T)\,|\,\mathcal{F}_t] = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[Y(T)\frac{\zeta(T)}{\zeta(t)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\right].
> $$
> 
> We now introduce the important concept of a deflator, a strictly positive Itô process used to normalize the asset prices. Let the deflator be denoted $D(t)$, and define the normalized asset process $X^D(t) = (X_1(t)/D(t),\dots,X_p(t)/D(t))^\top$. We say that a measure $\mathbb{Q}^D$ is an equivalent martingale measure induced by $D$ if $X^D(t)$ is a martingale with respect to $\mathbb{Q}^D$. If $\mathbb{Q}^D$ is a martingale measure, we say that a self-financing trading strategy is permissible if
> 
> $$
> \int_0^t \phi(s)^\top \,\mathrm{d}X^D_s
> $$
> 
> is a martingale.

> #### Theorem 1.3.2 (Sufficient condition for No-Arbitrage)
> 
> Restrict attention to permissible trading strategies. If there is a deflator $D$ such that the deflated asset price process admits an equivalent martingale measure, then there is no arbitrage.
> 
> If the deflator is one of the $p$ assets, we call the deflator a numéraire.
> 
> Let us assume that $X_1$ is strictly positive and can be used as a numéraire. Also assume that a deflator $D$ has been identified such that Theorem 1.3.2 holds. As $X_1(t)/D(t)$ is a $\mathbb{Q}^D$-martingale, we can use the Radon–Nikodym theorem to define a new measure $\mathbb{Q}^{X_1}$ by the density
> 
> $$
> \zeta(t) = \frac{X_1(t)/D(t)}{X_1(0)/D(0)}.
> $$
> 
> For an $\mathcal{F}_T$-measurable variable $Y(T)$, we then have:
> 
> $$
> X_1(t)\,\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}^{X_1}}\left[\frac{Y(T)}{X_1(T)}\right] = D(t)\,\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}^{D}}\left[\frac{Y(T)}{D(T)}\right].
> $$
> 
> In particular, if $Y(t)/D(t)$ is a $\mathbb{Q}^D$-martingale, $Y(t)/X_1(t)$ is a $\mathbb{Q}^{X_1}$-martingale.

## 1.4 Derivative Security Pricing and Complete Markets

A $T$-maturity derivative security (also known as a contingent claim) pays out at time $T$ an $\mathcal{F}_T$-measurable random variable $V(T)$, and makes no payments before $T$.

A trading strategy replicates the derivative security if $V(T)=\pi(T)$ almost surely, and more generally $V(t)=\pi(t)$ for all $t\in[0,T]$.

Specifically, consider a deflator $D$ and assume the existence of an equivalent martingale measure $\mathbb{Q}^D$ induced by $D$. It follows that

$$
\frac{V(t)}{D(t)} = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}^{D}}\left[\frac{V(T)}{D(T)}\right].
$$

> #### Theorem 1.4.1
> 
> In the absence of arbitrage, a market is complete if and only if there exists a deflator inducing a unique martingale measure.

> #### Theorem 1.4.2 (Change of Numeraire)
> 
> Consider two numeraires $N(t)$ and $M(t)$, inducing equivalent martingale measures $\mathbb{Q}^N$ and $\mathbb{Q}^M$, respectively. If the market is complete, then the density of the Radon–Nikodym derivative relating the two measures is uniquely given by
> 
> $$
> \zeta(t) = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}^N}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^M}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^N}\right] = \frac{M(t)/M(0)}{N(t)/N(0)}.
> $$

## 1.5 Girsanov's Theorem 

In this section, we consider : 
1) The conditions on the asset prices that allow for an equivalent martingale measure; 
2) The effect on asset dynamics from a chnage of probability measure. 

We consider two measures $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{P}(\theta)$ related by a density $\zeta^\theta(t)=\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right]$, where $\zeta^\theta(t)$ is an exponential martingale given by the Ito process

$$
\frac{d\zeta^\theta (t)}{\zeta^\theta(t)} = - \theta(t)^\top dW(t) 
$$

where $W(t)$ is a d-dimensional $\mathbb{P}$ Brownian motion. The d-dimensional process $\theta$ is known as the market price of risk. 

By an application of Ito's lemma, we can write 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\zeta^\theta(t) = \exp\left(-\int_0^t \theta(s)^\top dW(s) -\frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \theta(s)^\top \theta(s)ds\right) \\
= \epsilon\left( - \int_0^t \theta(s)^\top dW(s) \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\epsilon(.)$ is the Doleans exponential. 

> #### Theorem 1.5.1 (Girsanov's Theorem)
> 
> Suppose that $\zeta^\theta(t)$ is a martingale. Then for all $t\in [0, T]$
> 
> $$
> W^\theta(t) = W(t) + \int_0^t \theta(s)ds
> $$
>
> is a Brownian motion under the measure $\mathbb{P}(\theta)$

## 1.6 Stochastic Differential Equations

We consider a stochastic differential equation of the form

$$
dX(t) = \mu(t, X(t))dt + \sigma(t, X(t))dW(t), \quad X(0)=X_0
$$

> #### Theorem 1.6.1.
> In the SDE,
>
>$$ dX(t) = \mu(t, X(t))dt + \sigma(t, X(t))dW(t), \quad X(0)=X_0
>$$ 
>assume that there exists a constant $K$ such that for all $t \in[0, T]$ and all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^p$, 
>
>$$\begin{aligned}&|\mu(t,x)-\mu(t,y)| + |\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)| \leq K|x-y| \quad \text{Lipschitz condition}\\ &|\mu(t,x)|^2 + |\sigma(t,x)|\leq K^2(1+|x|^2) \quad \text{growth condition}\end{aligned}$$
> 
> Then there exists a unique solution to the SDE
